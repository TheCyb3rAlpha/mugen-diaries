THE SCORECARD · WHAT IT ACTUALLY CAUGHT

The scorecard

Every detection, sorted by how it was earned: blind and answer-hidden, or built to catch a bug that was already known. The distance between those two columns is the honest measure of the tool.

Compiled by Claude·from the project's own records·last updated 27 July 2026

A tool is worth exactly what it finds on something it has never seen. So this page keeps two kinds of result apart and never blurs them. The first is the blind test: real binaries, the known vulnerability hidden from the tool, no human steering. The second is the honest asterisk on every impressive demo, a detector built after the bug was known, aimed at the one binary it was meant to catch. Both are here. The gap is the story.

Blind and answer-hidden, MUGEN delivered zero complete source-to-sink chains on the three CVE sites checked by hand. Every end-to-end catch it can show was engineered against a binary whose bug was already known.

Three pictures of the same split. The first two describe the blind backtest; the third is the whole page in one frame: what the tool finds when it has never seen the answer, against what it finds when the answer was known in advance.

What became of the 41 acquired

Only eleven of the forty-one binaries could be anchored at all. Twenty-six were stripped past the point the tool could even name the vulnerable function, which describes most real software in the wild.

The gap · complete source-to-sink chains delivered

The distance between these two bars is the honest measure of the tool. Blind, it delivered zero complete source-to-sink chains. Every complete chain it can show was engineered to catch a bug that was already public.

Not one delivered a complete source-to-sink path to the operator. On the three sites checked by hand, the count was zero. And these eleven are the easy slice: the twenty-six that were dropped were stripped of symbols, which describes most real software in the wild. The zero-token figure is literal: this run generated the operator's prompts without ever calling the model. The model, when it does run, only re-checks what the static pipeline already surfaced, so it could not have rescued a site the pipeline missed.

A kernel driver, calibrated

A single real target where the answer was known in advance, run with the debug symbols the tool almost never has in the field.

Surfaced means the patched function reached the top of the pile. It does not mean a source-to-sink chain was drawn. Twelve surfaced, two fell past the ranking cut, and one missed on a name mismatch. And this ran with a version-matched symbol file that, by the project's own note, multiplies the detector's reach many times over what a stripped binary allows.

End-to-end, but not blind

End-to-end catches on genuine public exploit-database cases, delivered to the operator: at the #1 prompt in one case, lower in the queue or through a dedicated flag in the others. The caveat is the whole point of the section header. Each detector was built after the bug was known and aimed at that specific binary. This is "can the tool be made to catch a known bug end-to-end," not "point it at something new."

Read that table with the header in mind. Only the first, the access-control client, reaches the operator's #1 prompt. The chat server is delivered end-to-end but ranked #26. The FTP case is shakier still: the detector flagged a nearby overflow and missed the exact site the public exploit crashes. The last is detected but never delivered to a prompt at all. And neither of the two genuine deliveries is a solver proof; both are pushed into the operator's queue by a post-processor, the honest inverse of the false positive below, which the solver actually did prove. Every one of these detectors was written against a binary whose bug was already public.

One that wasn't real

Kept here on purpose. The most dangerous output a tool like this produces is a wrong answer that looks submittable.

CaseTargetClaimedResultTime

This one refutation is why a reachability filter exists at all. Turned loose on the backlog, it demoted 19 of 50 previously "Golden" prompts, and every one of the 19, checked by hand, was safe. The story is Post 10.
